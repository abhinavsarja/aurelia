Case Study — Proposed Solution

Smart Business Performance Dashboard

Business and technical assumptions for a retail performance dashboard with natural language analysis.

Client: AURELIA (illustrative) Market: Singapore Version 0.2 — working draft

—The brief

Senior management want a dashboard that shows overall business performance and lets them drill down to SKU level.

It must also answer questions in plain language, such as "Why did sales for this SKU drop last month?". Answers must be based on evidence from three sources:

  • Internal sales and operations data
  • Internal documents — campaign plans, meeting notes, operational reports
  • Public information — news, market events, competitor activity

1The business

We use a fictional company. Real retailers do not publish internal documents such as meeting notes, and those documents are central to this problem. Inventing the company keeps the example honest and lets us design a clear test case.

CompanyAURELIA — fictional
TypeAccessible-luxury fashion accessories. Similar in shape to Charles & Keith.
MarketSingapore only
ModelB2C — business to consumer. Sells directly to shoppers, not to other businesses.
ChannelsOwn e-commerce site and own retail stores. No wholesale.
Price rangeBags S$150–500. Jewellery S$60–250. Prices in SGD.
DepartmentsBags · Footwear · Jewellery · Eyewear · Small leather goods · Keychains
Product hierarchyDepartment → Model → SKU. Example: Bags → Marlow Crossbody → MRL-CB-TAN.
Catalogue sizeAround 60–70 models, giving roughly 250–350 SKUs once colours and sizes are counted.
HistoryThree years of weekly data

2Business assumptions

  1. The business operates only in Singapore and sells only to consumers (B2C).
  2. There are two channels: own e-commerce and own stores.
  3. All sales data is weekly. The week closes on Sunday. Data is available Monday morning.
  4. There is no need for live or real-time data.
  5. Targets are set monthly, not yearly. Performance is assessed over a four-week period against the monthly target.
  6. The system covers product performance only. There is no customer data and no personalisation.
  7. Once a week is closed, its numbers do not change. Returns are counted within the same week.
  8. Campaign and promotion timing comes from the internal documents, not from a separate calendar.

Where the monthly target comes from

The target is not calculated by this system. It is decided by management and written down in internal documents — monthly planning meeting minutes, buying committee notes, and similar records.

Targets are hardcoded values taken from documents For this solution, monthly targets are extracted from specific meeting minutes and planning documents and stored as fixed values. They are not connected to a live planning system. This is a deliberate simplification and should be stated openly.
  • A monthly sales target exists per department, and is broken down to model and SKU level.
  • Each month covers four weeks of sales data.
  • Targets are read once from the source documents and loaded into the system.
  • If management revises a target mid-month, it is reloaded manually.
Important caveat to state A target is more reliable at department level than at SKU level. A department target is a real commitment someone is accountable for. A SKU target is usually a calculated share that nobody personally agreed to. So a SKU missing target by 30% is weaker evidence than a department missing by 5%. The system should weight these differently.

3Users and decisions

UserWhat they look atHow often
CEO / CFOTotal sales and margin against the monthly targetMonthly, briefly
Commercial directorDepartment and model performance against targetWeekly
Buying and merchandisingSKU sell-through, stock cover, discount levelsSeveral times a week
MarketingCampaign impact on specific productsWeekly
Why drill-down to SKU matters, even though managers do not manage SKUs A CEO never decides what to do about one tan handbag. But when sales miss the monthly target by 6%, the only question that matters is whether it is one broken product, one weak category, or the whole business slowing down. Those three need completely different responses. The drill-down exists to answer that question. It is a way to check a number, not a tool for managing products.

The moment this is used

The monthly review meeting, and the weekly trading meeting that leads up to it. Someone asks why something dropped. Today that becomes a request to an analyst and takes one to two days. This system aims to answer it inside the meeting.

Decisions it supports

  • Reorder a product or not
  • When to discount, and by how much
  • Keep, drop or expand a model next season
  • Move stock between channels
  • Where to put campaign spend

4Scope

In scope

  • Performance dashboard, company level down to SKU
  • Comparison against the monthly target only
  • Natural language questions about past performance
  • Evidence-based explanations with sources shown

Out of scope

  • Forecasting future sales
  • Automatic reordering or stock decisions
  • Customer-level analysis or personalisation
  • Fixing data quality problems in source systems
  • Real-time or intraday data
  • Alerts or proactive lists of problem products
The system recommends, it never acts Every output is an explanation or a suggestion. A person makes the decision. This keeps accountability clear and greatly reduces risk.

5Business metrics shown

Performance is always compared against the monthly target. Four weeks of sales data are added together and measured against that month's number.

MetricWhy it matters
Net salesHeadline number. Compared against the monthly target.
Gross marginShows whether sales were profitable.
Full-price sell-throughThe most important metric in fashion. Shows what share of stock sold at full price. A business can hit its sales target while damaging itself through discounting. This number reveals that.
Average discountShows how much price is being given away.
Weeks coverHow many weeks of stock remain at the current sales rate. Flags both stock-outs and overstock.
Rate of saleUnits sold per week. Lets you compare products of different sizes fairly.

6Data model

Structured data — four tables

TableOne row perFields
ProductsSKUSKU · Model · Department · Colour · Size · Price · Cost · Launch date
SalesSKU, week, channelWeek · SKU · Channel · Units · Revenue · Discount %
StockSKU, weekWeek · SKU · Units on hand
TargetsSKU, monthMonth · SKU · Target units · Target revenue · Source document

Sales and stock are weekly. Targets are monthly. To compare them, four weeks of sales are added together and measured against that month's target.

The Source document field records which meeting minutes or planning document the target came from, so any number on screen can be traced back to where it was decided.

Stock is not split by channel. Everything the analysis needs — price effects, discount effects, stock-outs, channel differences, broken size ranges — can be worked out from these four tables.

Internal documents

Six to eight short documents for the prototype: a campaign plan, two buying meeting notes, a monthly planning meeting record containing the targets, a supply chain report, a returns summary.

Meeting notes are treated differently Meeting notes record decisions with dates. Rather than only splitting them into text chunks, we also pull out a decision list: who decided what, when, and which products it affected. This gives a timeline of decisions that can be placed next to the sales timeline. That is how cause becomes visible. For example: the reorder was cancelled on 12 May, stock ran out in week 24, sales fell in week 25.

Public information

Ten to fifteen news items with dates for the prototype. In production this would be a weekly feed of retail and fashion trade news for Singapore.

Deliberately kept small The data is small and the user count is low. Large-scale tools such as Snowflake, Kafka or a dedicated vector database would add cost and complexity with no benefit here. Most of the running cost will be language model calls, not servers.

Two things that are not memory, but are still needed

A business dictionary

Definitions of terms, the product hierarchy, and alternative names people use. For example, that "Marlow" is a model and "tan" is a colour. This is a fixed configuration file, not stored memory. Without it, questions cannot be interpreted reliably.

An answer cache

If two people ask the same question in the same week, they must get the same answer. Different answers to the same question would badly damage trust. Answers are stored and reused until new data arrives. This also reduces cost during the Monday peak.

Do the heavy work on Sunday night

Data arrives Sunday night. Questions come during the week. We use those hours to calculate the sales analysis for every SKU in advance. When a question is asked, the numbers are already there, so answers are much faster and more consistent.

8How an answer is built

The three sources are not searched at the same time. They run in order. Each step narrows the next.

  1. Understand the question. Work out which product and which period are meant. "Last month" becomes a specific set of four weeks. Show this back to the user so they can correct it.
  2. Do the maths. Run fixed calculations to split the gap against target into parts: how much came from being out of stock, from price changes, from lower demand, from a campaign ending. Whatever cannot be explained is recorded as a remainder.
  3. Search internal documents. Search using the findings from step 2, not the user's original question. Look for "Tan replenishment" and "Marlow" in May and June — not for "why did sales drop".
  4. Search public information — only if needed. Run this only when a large share of the gap is still unexplained. If stock-outs and campaigns already explain 86% of it, there is no need to search the news.
  5. Write the answer. Combine the evidence, label each piece by how reliable it is, and include a link to the SKU data for the period in question.

Why we do not use text-to-SQL for "why" questions

Text-to-SQL is good for "what" questions, such as "what were sales last week". It is the wrong tool for "why" questions.

Instead we use a set of fixed, tested calculations. The language model chooses which ones to run and with what inputs. It does not write the query.

ReasonExplanation
CorrectnessThe parts of the explanation must add up to the total gap. A generated query gives no such guarantee.
ConsistencyThe same question must give the same answer on Monday and on Wednesday.
TestabilityYou can test twelve fixed calculations. You cannot test every possible generated query.
SpeedFixed calculations can be optimised and pre-computed.

Text-to-SQL is still offered as a secondary option for open-ended exploration. It is read-only, limited in size, and clearly marked in the interface as unverified.

Public information needs a limit

This source will always find something Ask why bag sales fell, search the news, and you will always find a believable story — weaker consumer spending, fewer tourists, a competitor promotion. It sounds insightful and cannot be disproved. This is the biggest credibility risk in the system. So we only search the news when the internal data leaves a large part of the gap unexplained, and the answer is always allowed to say that no link was found.

For gathering the news, a search API is used at question time. Browser automation is too slow and unreliable for that. Browser automation is used separately as a weekly scheduled job to collect competitor prices and new product listings.

9Avoiding wrong answers

Wrong answers are the main risk. Senior management will act on what this system says. These controls exist to make errors visible rather than hidden.

ControlHow it works
The model never calculatesEvery number comes from a database query. The language model only explains numbers it is given. This is the single most important rule.
Fixed calculationsCause analysis uses tested code, not generated queries. Results are repeatable.
Numbers must add upThe parts of the explanation must sum to the total gap. If they do not, the answer is rejected before it is shown.
Everything is labelled by sourceThree levels: Measured from our data, Documented from internal files, External from public sources. Each looks different on screen.
Sources are clickableClicking a source opens the actual document at the exact paragraph. This is not just convenience — if a source is invented or does not support the claim, one click reveals it. It makes errors self-exposing.
Source check after writingA second automated pass confirms each cited passage actually supports the claim made.
Allowed to say "I don't know"When evidence is weak, the system says so. A system that always produces an explanation is inventing some of them.
Every answer is loggedThe question, the evidence used, and the answer are all saved. If someone later disputes an answer, we can show exactly what the system saw.

10Interface

The screen is split. Charts and tables on the left. The question and answer panel on the right. Everything on the left fits on one screen without scrolling.

Moving around the data

Three linked dropdowns: Department, then Style, then SKU. Each one loads only the items belonging to the level above it. Selecting a week and a channel filters everything on screen.

Link from an answer to the data

When the system answers a question about a SKU, the answer includes one link. Clicking it opens the dashboard showing that SKU for the period the question was about.

Keep it simple The chat does not control the dashboard and does not create charts. It gives the user one link so they can go and check the numbers themselves.
